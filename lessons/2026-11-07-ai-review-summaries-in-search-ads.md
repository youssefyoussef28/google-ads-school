## In 30 seconds

- Google can now add an **AI-written, positive-only review summary** to your **Search ads** in **English in the US, UK, Australia and India**. You **can't edit** the text.
- Google says it shows only when **predicted to improve performance**, but it has published **no results**, and the help page describes **no opt-out**.
- The practical lesson: your **reviews across the web are now part of your ad**. Fix your review footprint, and don't repeat in headlines what the summary will say for you.

## What changed

Google's Ads Help Center has a page called **"About Review summaries"** ([Google Ads Help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/18760807)). PPC News Feed first reported it on **October 24, 2026**, and PPC Land covered it the next day ([PPC Land](https://ppc.land/google-ai-writes-positive-only-review-summaries-into-search-ads-in-4-markets/)). What the page says:

- **What it is:** AI reads online reviews of a business and writes a short summary that appears as part of the Search ad. It highlights positive customer experiences.
- **Where:** Search campaigns, all ad positions on Google.com, all supported devices.
- **Markets:** the **US, UK, Australia and India**, in **English** only.
- **Sources:** reviews "from across the web". The page doesn't name the review sites.
- **Exclusions:** sensitive categories (pharmaceuticals is the only example given).
- **Control:** advertisers "cannot directly edit or customize the generated text". The page describes **no opt-out**.
- **When it shows:** only when Google predicts it will improve ad performance.

This isn't new from nowhere. PPC Land lists earlier tests Google ran: review excerpts in ads (December 2025), AI summaries under ad descriptions (July 2026), and labels like "What customers love" (September 2026).

> [!WARNING]
> The help page is undated and thin. It doesn't say how reviews are chosen, how a business with mixed reviews is handled, whether the ad is labeled as AI-generated, or whether summaries get their own reporting. Treat anything beyond the list above as unconfirmed.

## Why it matters

Until now, everything in your ad was something you wrote, or at least something you could see and remove. A summary of what *strangers* say about you, written by AI and placed inside your ad, is a different kind of asset. It can make your ads more persuasive. It also means your Google Business reviews, Trustpilot page and marketplace ratings can change your ad without you touching Google Ads.

## The lesson: ad copy now has three authors

Think of a Search ad as being written by three parties:

| Author | What they write | Your control |
|---|---|---|
| **You** | Headlines, descriptions, sitelinks, callouts | Full: write, pin, pause |
| **Google's automation** | Automatically created assets, AI Max text, dynamic text | Partial: account and campaign settings, text guidelines, removal of individual assets |
| **Your customers (via AI)** | Review summaries | None directly: you influence them by earning better reviews |

Two ideas follow:

1. **Don't spend your headlines on what the summary will say for you.** If a review summary can say "customers love the fast delivery", a headline that only says "Fast delivery" is wasted space. Use headlines for things reviews can't say: price, offer, location, guarantee, the exact product match.
2. **Your review footprint is now a conversion lever.** A business with a few hundred specific, recent reviews gives the AI better material than one with fifty vague ones. This is one of the few levers you still hold.

> [!NOTE]
> Google's text guidelines for AI Max and Performance Max (brand and wording restrictions) exist, but PPC Land points out the review summaries aren't mentioned in that framework. Don't assume the same controls cover them. Check in your own account.

## Worked example: a dental clinic in London

*Illustrative numbers, not real account data.*

**Brightside Dental** runs one Search campaign for "invisalign London" and spends £4,000 a month. Its reviews are spread across Google Business (310 reviews, 4.8 stars) and a smaller site with 40 reviews. Most Google reviews say things like "great" or "friendly staff", and few mention the thing patients actually buy: Invisalign.

Google's AI summarizes what it finds. With vague reviews, the summary is probably generic ("Patients praise the friendly team"). That adds little over what the ad says already.

**The fix, done over a few weeks:**
- Send a post-treatment review request that asks one specific question: "What did you come in for, and how did it go?"
- Reply to existing reviews and mention the treatment naturally ("Glad your Invisalign finished early").
- Move the Invisalign promise into headlines ("Free Invisalign consultation, £0") and let reviews carry the trust.

**How would they judge it?** There's no separate report for summaries, so they use a before/after comparison on the same campaign:

| | 4 weeks before | 4 weeks after |
|---|---|---|
| Impressions | 20,000 | 20,500 |
| CTR | 5.0% | 5.6% |
| Clicks | 1,000 | 1,148 |
| Conversion rate | 6.0% | 6.0% |
| Leads | 60 | 69 |
| CPA | £66.67 | £58.00 |

The numbers show *an improvement*, but they can't prove the summaries caused it, because the headline change, review requests and normal seasonality happened at the same time. That's the honest limit of the method.

> [!TIP]
> If you want a cleaner read, change one thing at a time: first the headlines, then wait 3–4 weeks, then the review push. Otherwise you'll never know which one worked.

## What others are doing

- **Seller ratings are the closest precedent.** In 2016, agency Metric Theory compared ads showing seller ratings to ads without across nine ecommerce accounts and saw CTR about **24% higher** and conversion rate about **26% higher**. It's an old, small, non-randomized comparison from one agency, and they note they couldn't run a true A/B test. Treat it as a hint that social proof helps, not a forecast. ([Metric Theory](https://metrictheory.com/blog/google-seller-ratings-a-paid-search-home-run/))
- **Practitioners who spotted the earlier tests** were the ones reporting these changes first. PPC Land credits individual advertisers for finding the December 2025, July 2026 and September 2026 tests ([PPC Land](https://ppc.land/google-ai-writes-positive-only-review-summaries-into-search-ads-in-4-markets/)). If you want early warning of the next change, those are the people to follow, along with the Search Engine Roundtable recaps linked in [Where to learn](resources.html).
- **Many accounts already turn off automatically created assets** as a policy. The videos below show where that setting lives. The help page doesn't say whether it covers review summaries, so don't rely on it.

## Where the experts disagree

**Google's view:** the summaries build trust before the click, improve click quality and are shown only when predicted to help performance. They follow "responsible AI practices and grounding requirements".

**The skeptics' view:** it's AI-written content with your name on it, positive-only by design, that you can't edit and may not be able to turn off. PPC Land raises the question of how positive-only summaries compiled by a platform fit with the FTC's rule on fake and misleading reviews, which the help page doesn't address. It also cites a survey of 565 US marketers in which 47% said they see AI inaccuracies weekly ([PPC Land](https://ppc.land/google-ai-writes-positive-only-review-summaries-into-search-ads-in-4-markets/)).

**Where the evidence is thin:** Google has published no uplift numbers for review summaries specifically. The only performance data I found is a 2016 agency study on a different feature.

**Our take:** For most advertisers with genuinely good reviews, this is more likely to help than hurt, and the best response is to improve what the AI can read. If your reviews are mixed, or you work somewhere where a wrong claim is costly (health, finance, legal), watch your ads closely: search for your own ads, look for summaries that overstate or misstate, and ask your Google rep about opt-out. Don't assume an account setting turns this off until Google says so.

## Do this today

1. **Search for your own ads** on google.com from a US, UK, Australian or Indian location (or ask a colleague there) and look for a review summary. Take a screenshot if you find one.
2. **Read your reviews the way the AI will.** Look at your five most recent review sources. Are they specific? Do any mention the product you advertise?
3. **Ask for specific reviews:** change your review-request message to ask what they bought and how it went.
4. **Rework headlines** so they carry price, offer, location or guarantee, not generic praise.
5. **Check your asset settings:** in your account, open the settings for automatically created assets and note whether they're on. The help page doesn't confirm this controls review summaries, so treat it as a hygiene check.
6. **Ask your Google rep** (or Support) two questions: is there an opt-out for review summaries, and will they be reportable?
7. **Write a baseline:** CTR, conversion rate and CPA for your main Search campaigns today, so you can compare in 4–6 weeks.
